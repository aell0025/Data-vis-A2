Objects of Devotion

What fans keep, and why. A look at the physical artefacts of music fandom — from pressed vinyl to concert ephemera — held in Australian museum collections and the global record-collecting community.

Why this matters

A concert poster, a signed sleeve, a limited-edition pressing: music leaves objects behind, and the ones people choose to keep say something about what a band or a song meant to them. This page asks what music memorabilia gets kept, when it was made, and where it comes from — by a public museum and by record collectors.

It combines two sources: 142 music objects from the Museums Victoria collection, and 153 physical releases by 35 artists from Discogs, the world’s largest record database. No background is needed. Scroll down for the museum, then the record shelf, then the two side by side.

Part one: in the museum

142 music objects held by Museums Victoria.

What gets kept

Among the music memorabilia held by Museums Victoria, physical disc recordings dominate — but a long tail of medals, sheet music, posters and letters tells a quieter story about what people valued enough to donate to a public collection.

Only object types with 2 or more items shown.

How the museum files it

More than half the items (77 of 142) sit under “Music & musical instruments”. Each block is an object type, and its size is the number of items. Disc recordings are the single largest block, with 49.

Colour shows the museum’s own classification. The squares below show the same items, one square each, with each classification’s share of the 142. Where an item has several, the first of eight music classifications listed in the written submission is used.

When it was made

Collecting peaks in the 1930s, 1950s and 1980s. Early decades are mostly records and sheet music; later decades add a wider mix of objects.

Click a legend entry to highlight a kind of object. Each dot is one item. 113 of 142 items have an estimated year; 26 have none and 3 pre-date 1870, so they are not shown. Years are approximate.

Kept close to home

Of the 83 Museums Victoria items linked to an Australian place, 67 belong to Greater Melbourne, led by the city itself and the inner suburb of Carlton. Circles are sized by the number of items; inner-city suburbs overlap because they sit only a few kilometres apart.

Shaded suburbs have at least one item. Another 16 Australian items are tied to places outside this view, mainly Sydney.

Objects that travelled

14 items in the collection trace back to places overseas — London, Paris, Chicago, Calcutta and more. Each line runs from the place an item is linked to, to Melbourne, where it ended up.

Line thickness shows the number of items. Lines are straight paths on the map, not flight routes.

Part two: on the record shelf

153 physical releases by 35 artists, from Discogs (digital-only releases removed). Each release is grouped into a pressing tier: standard, reissue, or special (limited, numbered, picture disc, box set, promo or test pressing).

Pressed to be collected

Not every release is made to be kept. Special pressings rise and fall across decades, layered here against ordinary and reissue pressings.

Streamgraph: release count by decade and pressing tier. Click a legend entry to highlight that tier; click it again to reset.

Where collector pressings are made

Of the 145 sampled releases with a known country, 87 — 60% — were pressed in Japan. Japanese reissues of Western albums are prized for their sound quality and packaging, so the map shows where collectors’ favourite editions come from, not where the bands do.

Darker means more releases. Releases listed as “Worldwide”, “Europe” or “Unknown” (8 of 153) can’t be placed on a country map; “USSR” is shown as Russia. Use the buttons to show only special pressings: Japan accounts for 32 of the 48 special pressings with a known country.

The sound of each decade

Synth-pop owns the 1980s, with eight sampled releases, alternative rock peaks in the 1990s, and pop rap leads the 2000s with six. Genres have “styles” — finer labels like soul or electro — and the hotter the cell, the more releases of that style from that decade.

Twelve most common styles shown. A release can carry more than one style.

Waves of genres

Rock peaks around 1990, hip hop is mostly a 2000s story (15 of its 25 sampled releases), and electronic music has two humps, one in the 1980s and a later one around 2010. Taller means more releases from that year.

Smoothed counts of release year for the three most common main genres. Reflects the 35 artists sampled, not all music.

Wanted more than owned

For a standard pressing, about 1.4 collectors want a copy for every one who owns it. For reissues the figure is 3.2, and for special pressings 2.7. Scarcity, or the promise of it, pulls collectors in.

Each dot is one release; the dark bar is the middle value for its tier. Log scale: each gridline is ten times the last. Releases nobody has listed as wanted or owned are left out.

Who gets the special treatment

25 of the 35 artists have at least one special pressing in the sample. AC/DC and Amy Winehouse lead, with four of five sampled releases each; Run-D.M.C. and Radiohead have none.

Click a legend entry to highlight a tier. Up to five releases were sampled per artist, so this shows what the sample found, not each artist’s full catalogue.

From decade to pressing

Follow any decade to its genres, and on to how the releases were pressed. Electronic is the most likely of the three biggest genres to get a special pressing (16 of 44, 36%), ahead of hip hop (8 of 25) and rock (18 of 61). The 1990s stand out: only 2 of 22 releases from that decade are special pressings, against 12 of 29 in the 1980s.

Part three: two shelves, side by side

The museum’s objects and the record sample, compared decade by decade.

Different shelves, different eras

The museum’s shelf reaches back to the 1870s, and 71 of its 113 dated items are older than 1960. The record sample starts in the 1960s, because of the artists chosen. Where the two overlap, museum objects peak in the 1980s while the records peak in the 2000s.

Each side adds up to 100% of its own source. Museum years are estimated from item records; record years are release years, including reissues.
